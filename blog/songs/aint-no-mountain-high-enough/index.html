Ain't No Mountain High Enough: Diana Ross's 1970 Hit

By Charlie · Published October 19, 2026

▶ Jump to the video

Ain't No Mountain High Enough was a modest hit in 1967 and a number one smash in 1970.

Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell took it to number 19, then Diana Ross took her version to the top of the Billboard Hot 100.

Her record spent three weeks at number one in September and October 1970.

Below are the writers, the original, the spoken intro Berry Gordy hated, the chart run and the covers.

Ain't No Mountain High Enough: From Ashford and Simpson to Diana Ross

Written in 1966 for Tamla

According to Wikipedia, Nickolas Ashford and Valerie Simpson wrote the song in 1966 for Tamla, a division of Motown.

Dusty Springfield wanted to record it.

The duo held it back to gain entry to Motown instead.

The Gaye and Terrell Original

Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell recorded the first version between December 1966 and February 1, 1967.

Harvey Fuqua and Johnny Bristol produced it, and Tamla released it on April 20, 1967, from the album United.

It reached number 19 on the Hot 100 and number 3 on the R&B chart.

You can follow Gaye's solo career in our post on Marvin Gaye.

Ross Recasts It in 1970

Diana Ross recorded her version in March 1970 at Hitsville USA in Detroit.

Ashford and Simpson produced it themselves, with Paul Riser arranging.

The album cut runs 6:18, long enough for a slow, spoken build before the chorus lifts.

That build is the part that made Berry Gordy wince.

He disliked the record on first hearing, especially the spoken-word passages, and wanted the chorus up front.

Ashford and Simpson persuaded him to release an edited three-minute single after radio stations made their own edits.

On the Charts: Three Weeks at Number One

Ross's First Solo Chart-Topper

This site's weekly Hot 100 data has the song at number one for the chart weeks starting September 19, September 26 and October 3, 1970.

That is three weeks, and it was Ross's first number one as a solo act after leaving the Supremes.

Wikipedia also lists it at number one on the Cash Box Top 100 and the Billboard soul chart.

It beat the Gaye and Terrell version by eighteen places on the Hot 100.

Honors, Covers and Revivals

Grammy Recognition

Wikipedia lists these honors across the two recordings:

  • Diana Ross version: nominated for the Grammy for Best Female Pop Vocal Performance.
  • Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell version: inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1999.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who wrote Ain't No Mountain High Enough?

Nickolas Ashford and Valerie Simpson wrote Ain't No Mountain High Enough in 1966, according to Wikipedia.

Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell released the first version on April 20, 1967.

Diana Ross recorded her own version in March 1970, with Ashford and Simpson producing.

Did Ain't No Mountain High Enough reach number one?

Diana Ross's version of Ain't No Mountain High Enough reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100, according to Wikipedia.

This site's weekly chart data shows it on top for three weeks, from September 19 to October 9, 1970.

The Gaye and Terrell original peaked at number 19.

Who sang Ain't No Mountain High Enough first?

Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell sang Ain't No Mountain High Enough first, on a single released April 20, 1967, according to Wikipedia.

Their version reached number 19 on the Billboard Hot 100 and number 3 on the R&B chart.

Diana Ross recorded it three years later.

Why did Berry Gordy dislike Diana Ross's Ain't No Mountain High Enough?

Berry Gordy disliked Diana Ross's Ain't No Mountain High Enough on first hearing, according to Wikipedia, especially its spoken-word passages.

He wanted it to open with the chorus and bridge.

Ashford and Simpson persuaded him to release an edited three-minute single after radio stations made their own edits.

What awards did Ain't No Mountain High Enough receive?

Diana Ross's Ain't No Mountain High Enough was nominated for the Grammy for Best Female Pop Vocal Performance, according to Wikipedia.

The earlier Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell recording, released in 1967, was inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1999, long after its original run.
