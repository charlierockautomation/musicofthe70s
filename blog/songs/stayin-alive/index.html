Stayin Alive: Built on a Magazine Story That Was Fake

Stayin alive was written for a movie based on a true story that turned out not to be true at all.

Robert Stigwood handed the Bee Gees a vague brief about disco New York before a script even existed.

Barry Gibb answered with a falsetto he'd only just discovered by accident.

The song hit number one within weeks.

Decades later, doctors started using its beat to teach people CPR.

Stayin Alive Was Built on a Fake Magazine Story

The Magazine Article That Wasn't True

Stayin alive exists because of a New York magazine story that never actually happened the way it was written.

Journalist Nik Cohn published "Tribal Rites of the New Saturday Night" in June 1976.

It read as straight reporting on a real Brooklyn disco scene and a real young regular named Vincent.

Producer Robert Stigwood bought the film rights on the strength of that article alone.

Twenty years later, Cohn admitted the truth.

He'd struggled to find real material, gotten sick outside a Bay Ridge club on his one research trip, and gone home to invent the rest.

Vincent was fiction, built from a London mod Cohn had known years earlier.

The movie that became Saturday Night Fever, and the song written for it, both trace back to a story its own author later called mostly made up.

Stigwood Handed the Gibbs a Vague Brief, No Movie Yet

The Bee Gees wrote stayin alive before Saturday Night Fever even had a finished script.

Stigwood gave them almost nothing to work with, just a description of gritty disco nightlife and a story about a young man trying to matter.

The brothers wrote most of the soundtrack over a few days at Chateau d'Herouville studio outside Paris.

Barry Gibb said the song grew out of imagining what it felt like to be famous purely for how you danced.

RSO Records released it in December 1977 as the soundtrack's second single.

The film's opening shot, John Travolta walking a real Brooklyn street to that beat, became one of the most recognized entrances in movie history.

The Falsetto Barry Gibb Found by Accident

A Discovery Made on a Different Song First

The voice that carries stayin alive wasn't part of Barry Gibb's original range.

He found it earlier, recording "Nights on Broadway" for the Main Course album.

Producer Arif Mardin asked him to try a chorus part higher than felt natural.

Barry pushed into falsetto almost as a joke.

It worked, and he realized he could hold an entire song up there, not just a line.

That accidental discovery became the vocal signature of the whole disco era that followed.

One Line Where Barry Drops the Falsetto

Barry sings nearly all of stayin alive in that same falsetto.

He drops down to his natural register for exactly one line, "life going nowhere, somebody help me."

The dip lands right where the lyric turns desperate, then the falsetto snaps back for the chorus.

Robin and Maurice Gibb layer close harmony underneath him the rest of the way.

Producer Karl Richardson built the rhythm track around a looped drum pattern, since drummer Dennis Byron couldn't get a full bar clean in one take.

That loop became the steady, mechanical pulse the whole song rides on.

Chart Number One, Then a Second Life in CPR Class

Four Weeks at Number One

Stayin alive hit number one on the Billboard Hot 100 the week of February 4, 1978.

It held the top spot for four straight weeks.

It was one of an eight-song run of number ones the Bee Gees either recorded or wrote that year.

This site's own year-end Hot 100 archive lists it among 1978's defining hits.

It took a full year longer to collect its Grammy.

Stayin alive won Best Arrangement for Voices at the 1979 ceremony and picked up a Record of the Year nomination.

The Saturday Night Fever soundtrack itself took Album of the Year the same night.

103 Beats a Minute, the Same Rate CPR Needs

Stayin alive found an entirely new audience decades after it left the charts.

The song runs at roughly 103 beats per minute.

The American Heart Association recommends chest compressions at 100 to 120 beats per minute during CPR.

A University of Illinois College of Medicine study found that responders trained using the song's rhythm held a steadier compression rate, both immediately and months later.

A song written for a fictionalized magazine story now doubles as a real metronome that helps save lives.

Frequently Asked Questions

What inspired the stayin alive song?

The stayin alive song was written for Saturday Night Fever, a movie built on a 1976 New York magazine article about Brooklyn disco culture.

Writer Nik Cohn later admitted in 1997 that the article was largely fabricated, including its central character.

Robert Stigwood commissioned songs from the Bee Gees before the film even had a script.

Why does Barry Gibb sing falsetto on stayin alive?

Barry Gibb discovered his falsetto by accident while recording a different song, Nights on Broadway, when producer Arif Mardin asked him to try singing a chorus part higher.

He carried that voice into stayin alive and sang the entire song in falsetto except for one line, "life going nowhere, somebody help me."

How many weeks was stayin alive number one?

The song reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 the week of February 4, 1978, and held the top spot for four straight weeks.

It anchored a run of Bee Gees and Bee Gees-written songs that dominated 1978's charts.

This site's own year-end archive lists it among that year's biggest hits.

Why do doctors use stayin alive for CPR?

Doctors use stayin alive to teach CPR because it runs at roughly 103 beats per minute, close to the 100 to 120 chest compressions per minute recommended by the American Heart Association.

A University of Illinois study found responders trained with the song kept a steadier compression rate.

Its title also happens to fit the moment perfectly.

Did stayin alive win a Grammy?

Stayin alive itself won Best Arrangement for Voices at the 1979 Grammy Awards and was also nominated for Record of the Year.

The wider Saturday Night Fever soundtrack won Album of the Year at the same ceremony.

Those wins came a full year after the song had already finished its chart run.
