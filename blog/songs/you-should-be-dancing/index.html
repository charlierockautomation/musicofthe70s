You Should Be Dancing: Where Barry Gibb's Falsetto Began

You Should Be Dancing gave the Bee Gees their third US number one in September 1976.

It also gave Barry Gibb something brand new: his first full falsetto lead on a major single.

Keyboardist Blue Weaver had been pushing the group toward a real disco arrangement for months.

A rock star working in the studio next door ended up playing percussion on the finished track.

Stephen Stills wasn't even supposed to be part of the session.

This is the real recording and chart story, verified against Billboard data and outside reporting.

Blue Weaver Pushed the Bee Gees Toward Disco

The Bee Gees had already started shifting toward R&B and dance rhythms the year before.

That shift began on the Bee Gees single Jive Talkin.

You Should Be Dancing pushed that shift the rest of the way, into a full disco arrangement.

The song came from the 1976 album Children of the World.

Barry, Robin, and Maurice Gibb wrote it together.

The Bee Gees produced it with Albhy Galuten and Karl Richardson.

Keyboardist Blue Weaver had wanted to build a real disco arrangement for a while.

This track was where he finally got to try it.

Maurice Sang the Horn Parts Before Anyone Played Them

Maurice Gibb built the song's bass line himself.

He then sang the horn parts note for note to the brass players.

That way, they'd play exactly what he heard in his head.

Barry did the same thing in reverse, singing lines for Blue Weaver to translate onto the keyboard.

None of it started on paper.

The whole arrangement passed through the brothers' own voices before a single instrument confirmed it.

That's an unusual way to build a song.

It's closer to a vocal group scatting out a horn chart than a band writing sheet music.

Two Studios, One Falsetto, and a Cameo From Stephen Stills

Recording stretched across four separate sessions in early 1976: January 19, February 1, February 8, and May 6.

The group split the work between two studios.

  • Criteria Studios in Miami, Florida
  • Le Studio in Morin Heights, Quebec

Barry Gibb sang the lead in full falsetto.

It was the first time that style carried an entire major Bee Gees single.

It's the same voice that would define nearly every hit the group had for the rest of the decade.

The Neighbor Who Ended Up on the Track

Stephen Stills was recording a Crosby, Stills & Nash album in the studio right next door at Criteria.

He heard the Bee Gees' session bleeding through the wall and knew it was something different.

Stills later described that moment directly.

"I'd heard some of that Saturday Night Fever stuff and I knew it was totally unique and going to be a monster. So I played timbales and for a long time that was my only platinum single."

That's a rock guitarist best known for Crosby, Stills & Nash.

Yet there he was, playing percussion on a disco record almost by accident.

You Should Be Dancing Topped Two Different Billboard Charts

You Should Be Dancing reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 for the week of September 4, 1976, confirmed against this site's own weekly chart data.

It held that top spot for one week and finished the year ranked 31st on Billboard's year-end Hot 100 for 1976.

That made it the Bee Gees' third US number one.

How Can You Mend a Broken Heart came first in 1971, then Jive Talkin in 1975.

Music of the 70s tracks that same kind of chart progression across every act in its database.

The First Number One on a Brand New Chart

Billboard launched a new National Disco Action Top 30 chart with the issue dated August 28, 1976.

You Should Be Dancing became the very first number one in that chart's history.

It held the top spot there for five weeks.

It also reached number 5 on the UK Singles Chart and number 4 on Billboard's Soul chart.

How You Should Be Dancing Ended Up in Saturday Night Fever

Saturday Night Fever arrived in theaters in December 1977.

That was more than a year after this song's own chart run ended.

The film's soundtrack used six different Bee Gees songs, and this was one of them.

Stream You Should Be Dancing on this site's own 70s jukebox before watching how it landed on screen.

The Scene Travolta Refused to Let the Studio Cut Short

The song plays during the film's dance-floor scene at the fictional 2001 Odyssey club.

John Travolta's character leaves his partner mid-song and takes over the floor solo while the whole club watches.

Travolta had insisted on that exact song, since it was the only one he'd fully rehearsed to.

He reportedly threatened to walk off the film over it.

The studio had wanted to shoot the scene in close-up instead of full body.

Frequently Asked Questions

What was different about Barry Gibb's vocal on You Should Be Dancing?

You Should Be Dancing gave Barry Gibb his first full falsetto lead on a major Bee Gees single.

That vocal style had only appeared in pieces on earlier tracks before this one.

It became the group's signature sound for the rest of the decade.

That same sound carried straight through into the Saturday Night Fever soundtrack a year later.

Did Stephen Stills really play on You Should Be Dancing?

Yes, Stephen Stills played timbales on the finished track.

He was recording a Crosby, Stills & Nash album in the studio next door at Criteria in Miami.

He asked to sit in after hearing the Bee Gees' session through the wall.

Stills later called it the only platinum single he had for a long time.

How high did You Should Be Dancing chart, and was it a number one?

You Should Be Dancing reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 for one week in September 1976.

That made it the Bee Gees' third US chart-topper.

It also became the first ever number one on Billboard's new National Disco Action chart, holding that top spot for five weeks.

On top of that, it peaked at number 5 in the UK.

How is You Should Be Dancing connected to Saturday Night Fever?

He reportedly threatened to quit rather than let the studio film it in close-up instead of full body.
