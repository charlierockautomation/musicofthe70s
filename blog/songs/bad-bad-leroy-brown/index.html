Bad Bad Leroy Brown: The Only Number One Jim Croce Saw Hit

Bad Bad Leroy Brown gave Jim Croce his first number one single.

It was also the only one he lived to hear on the radio.

The character came from a real soldier Croce met during Army National Guard training.

The record topped the Hot 100 for two weeks in July 1973.

It was still charting when Croce died in a plane crash that September.

This is the chart-verified story, built from this site's own Billboard data and outside chart history.

The Real Leroy Brown Jim Croce Met in the Army

Jim Croce spent years working odd jobs and playing bars before this song ever reached a chart.

He told the story of its inspiration the same way most times he introduced it live.

A Soldier at Fort Dix

Croce met the real Leroy Brown during Army National Guard training at Fort Dix.

The man talked a big game and was feared by the rest of the regiment.

One night Brown decided he didn't like it there anymore, so he went AWOL.

Like the character in the finished song, the real Brown got his comeuppance for all that talk.

Moving the Story to Chicago

Croce didn't set the song at Fort Dix.

He moved it to the South Side of Chicago instead.

The character's swagger came from a different set of people entirely.

Croce once sold radio airtime around Philadelphia.

That job put him in the pool rooms and bars of South and West Philly.

Those bigger-than-life regulars shaped how "the baddest man in the whole damn town" actually talks and moves.

Recording Bad Bad Leroy Brown for Life and Times

ABC Records released Bad Bad Leroy Brown as a single on March 20, 1973.

It came from Croce's album "Life and Times," recorded the year before with producers Terry Cashman and Tommy West.

Croce's regular guitarist, Maury Muehleisen, played on the sessions that produced the album.

The two had built a close working partnership by that point.

That partnership shaped most of Croce's biggest hits from that era.

Where Bad Bad Leroy Brown Landed on the Charts

Bad Bad Leroy Brown topped the Billboard Hot 100 for two weeks.

Those were the chart weeks of July 21 and July 28, 1973.

This site's own weekly chart data confirms both weeks directly.

The song did the same on Cash Box's competing Top 100 chart.

It also reached number one on Canada's RPM Top Singles chart that summer.

Overseas the reach was smaller.

It peaked at number 11 in Australia, with a modest showing on the Dutch charts.

A Strong Year-End Finish

On Billboard's year-end chart for 1973, Bad Bad Leroy Brown finished at number 2.

That put it one spot behind Tony Orlando and Dawn's "Tie a Yellow Ribbon Round the Ole Oak Tree."

Cash Box's own year-end recap placed it at number 2 as well.

The Chart Run Croce Was Alive to See

The two weeks at number one turned out to matter more than anyone realized at the time.

Jim Croce died on September 20, 1973.

A small plane carrying him crashed on takeoff from Natchitoches, Louisiana, after a concert at Northwestern State University.

Bad Bad Leroy Brown was still sitting on the Hot 100 the week he died.

It remains the last song Croce is known to have publicly performed.

The Song He Never Got to Hear at the Top

Croce had two number one singles on the Hot 100.

The timing split them in a way that still stands out.

Bad Bad Leroy Brown hit number one that July, while he was alive to hear it himself.

His second number one, Time in a Bottle, didn't top the chart until that December and January.

That was three months after his death, once ABC re-released it as a single.

The Mood Song Matcher still tags Croce's catalog nostalgic and reflective.

That mood took on new weight once listeners knew how the year actually ended.

Grammy Recognition and What Came After

Bad Bad Leroy Brown picked up two nominations at the 1974 Grammy Awards, the ceremony covering the 1973 recording year.

It was nominated for Record of the Year and for Best Male Pop Vocal Performance.

Neither one went Croce's way.

  • Record of the Year went to Roberta Flack and producer Joel Dorn for "Killing Me Softly with His Song."
  • Best Male Pop Vocal Performance went to Stevie Wonder for "You Are the Sunshine of My Life."

The song still carries one odd bit of chart trivia beyond the nominations.

It was only the second number one single on the Hot 100 to include a curse word.

"Theme from Shaft" was the first, a year earlier.

A Folk-Rock Storyteller's Biggest Hit

Croce built his catalog on character-driven story songs.

This one became the biggest of them commercially.

It sits alongside the wider 70s soft rock scene.

That scene gave working-class narrative songwriting a real home on pop radio.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the real story behind Bad Bad Leroy Brown?

Jim Croce based Leroy Brown on a soldier he met during Army National Guard training at Fort Dix, a man who talked a big game and was feared around the regiment until he went AWOL.

Croce moved the story's setting to the South Side of Chicago, giving the character the swagger of tough guys he'd met in Philadelphia pool rooms and bars while selling radio airtime.

Did Bad Bad Leroy Brown reach number one on the Billboard Hot 100?

Bad Bad Leroy Brown topped the Billboard Hot 100 for two weeks in July 1973.

It also hit number one on the Cash Box Top 100 and Canada's RPM Top Singles chart that same summer, and finished at number 2 on Billboard's year-end chart for 1973.

Was Jim Croce still alive when Bad Bad Leroy Brown hit number one?

Jim Croce was alive and touring when Bad Bad Leroy Brown reached number one in July 1973.

He died in a plane crash on September 20, 1973, two months later, while the song was still on the Hot 100, making it the only number one single he lived to see.

Did Bad Bad Leroy Brown win a Grammy?

Bad Bad Leroy Brown earned two nominations at the 1974 Grammy Awards, for Record of the Year and Best Male Pop Vocal Performance, but it did not win either.

Roberta Flack's "Killing Me Softly with His Song" took Record of the Year, and Stevie Wonder won Best Male Pop Vocal Performance for "You Are the Sunshine of My Life."

How is Bad Bad Leroy Brown different from Time in a Bottle?

Bad Bad Leroy Brown became Jim Croce's first number one in July 1973 while he was alive to hear it on the radio.

Time in a Bottle became his second number one that December and January, after ABC Records re-released it following his death, a posthumous hit he never got to see.
