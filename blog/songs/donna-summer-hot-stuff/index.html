Donna Summer Hot Stuff: The Rock Turn That Won a Grammy

Donna Summer Hot Stuff reached number one in June 1979, lost the top spot after a week, then climbed back and held it.

By then Summer was the biggest disco star in the world.

She also wanted out of that label.

The single that broke her free had a hard rock guitar solo bolted onto a dance track.

The guitarist bought his instrument that day from a clearance bin for about 35 dollars.

A year later the record won the first Grammy ever handed out for Best Female Rock Vocal Performance.

A disco singer took it ahead of Bonnie Raitt, Carly Simon and Rickie Lee Jones.

This is the chart-verified story, built from Billboard's own weekly and year-end data and cross-checked against independent chart history.

Donna Summer Wanted a Rock Record, Not Another Disco Single

By 1979 Donna Summer had spent four years as the voice of disco.

Giorgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte produced every one of her hits from a studio in Munich.

The formula worked, and it also boxed her in.

Summer wanted to sing rock, and she said so out loud.

Her seventh studio album, Bad Girls, was where she pushed for it.

Casablanca released the double album in April 1979.

Rolling Stone later called it the only great disco album other than the Saturday Night Fever soundtrack.

Summer kept Moroder and Bellotte instead of hiring rock producers.

They knew her voice and how far it could stretch.

The Writers Behind the Song

Three people wrote Donna Summer Hot Stuff, and none of them was Summer or Moroder.

The credit went to a team that would go on to shape 1980s pop:

  • Pete Bellotte, Summer's longtime collaborator, who also produced the track with Moroder.
  • Harold Faltermeyer, who later wrote the synthesizer theme "Axel F" for Beverly Hills Cop.
  • Keith Forsey, Summer's drummer, who later co-wrote "Flashdance... What a Feeling" and produced "Don't You (Forget About Me)".

The song was reportedly shown to other singers first, including Cher.

Summer recorded it instead.

The $35 Guitar Solo That Made Donna Summer Hot Stuff a Rock Song

The rock in the record is mostly one guitar part.

It belongs to Jeff "Skunk" Baxter, formerly of Steely Dan and the Doobie Brothers.

Baxter was moving house when the session came up.

All of his equipment was packed and in transit.

He had no guitar to bring.

One Take on a Clearance-Bin Guitar

Baxter walked into a Guitar Center in Hollywood and asked for something cheap.

The manager pointed him at a clearance box.

He picked a Burns Baby Bison with mismatched tuning pegs for about 35 dollars.

He grabbed a six-pack of beer on the way out.

That bargain guitar is the one you hear on Hot Stuff when the site's jukebox plays the single.

At Rusk Sound Studios he plugged into a Fender Deluxe Reverb amp.

He turned everything up to ten and asked the engineer to roll tape.

"And that was it," Baxter said later.

"It was one take."

Why the Solo Sounds a Little Off

Baxter almost turned the session down.

He had been doing so much formula disco work that the parts had started to blur together.

He agreed only after Moroder promised him room to do something different.

The guitar he bought would not hold its tuning.

That is part of why the solo has a sharp, slightly sour edge against the clean dance track.

The tension between the two is the whole point of the record.

Where Donna Summer Hot Stuff Landed on the Charts

The single entered the Billboard Hot 100 in the middle of April 1979.

It reached number one for the week of June 2, 1979.

The site's own weekly Billboard data logs it at the top that week.

Then the Bee Gees knocked it off with "Love You Inside Out".

Donna Summer Hot Stuff dropped to number two for one week.

It returned to number one for the weeks of June 16 and June 23.

Three Weeks at Number One, With a Gap

That adds up to three weeks on top, split by a single week in the middle.

The site's weekly data records the interruption instead of rounding it to a clean run.

Most chart summaries just say "three non-consecutive weeks" and leave it there.

Billboard's published chart history adds one more figure.

The single spent 14 weeks inside the Hot 100 top ten.

Billboard lists that as the longest top-ten run of any 1979 release.

The Year-End Picture

On the site's own 1979 year-end Hot 100, Donna Summer Hot Stuff finished at number seven.

The wider run made Summer the top-performing chart act of that year.

One place above it on that year-end list sat Gloria Gaynor's I Will Survive.

The Grammy a Disco Record Was Not Supposed to Win

Nothing about Hot Stuff reads as rock on the surface.

The Mood Song Matcher would group it with the decade's dance records, not its guitar acts.

Then the 1980 Grammy Awards added a new category: Best Female Rock Vocal Performance.

Donna Summer Hot Stuff won the first one ever awarded.

The other nominees were all rock or singer-songwriter acts:

  • Rickie Lee Jones
  • Tanya Tucker
  • Carly Simon
  • Bonnie Raitt

Set against the rest of the decade on Music of the 70s, a disco single taking a rock award still looks like a fluke.

The site's own year-end Billboard data calls it the first disco record to win a rock Grammy.

What Donna Summer Brought to It

Summer's husband, Bruce Sudano, put the performance down to instinct.

"She was an actress who sang," Sudano said.

She gave the song the rock and soul it asked for.

She had pushed for a rock record, and then she sang like she meant it.

Why Donna Summer Hot Stuff Still Turns Up Everywhere

The disco backlash hit hard in the second half of 1979.

Radio stations dropped the word almost overnight.

Hot Stuff mostly slipped past it, because it already had one foot in rock.

The same disco-to-rock shift plays out across the back half of the decade on this site.

The song kept working long after the format that made it fell away.

A Second Life on Screen

The song kept finding new audiences long after disco faded:

  • It scores the job-club scene in the 1997 film The Full Monty.
  • It plays again in The Martian in 2015.
  • The Pussycat Dolls built their 2005 single "Hot Stuff (I Want You Back)" on a sample of it.
  • Decades of television commercials have leaned on the same hook.

For a record made to break its singer out of one genre, Donna Summer Hot Stuff did exactly that.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who played the guitar solo on Donna Summer's Hot Stuff?

The guitar solo on "Hot Stuff" was played by Jeff "Skunk" Baxter, a former member of Steely Dan and the Doobie Brothers.

Baxter was between homes and had no guitar with him for the session.

He bought a clearance model for about 35 dollars and cut the solo in one take.

The recording happened at Rusk Sound Studios in Los Angeles.

How many weeks was Hot Stuff number one?

"Hot Stuff" spent three weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in June 1979, but not in consecutive weeks.

It topped the chart for the week of June 2, then lost the spot to the Bee Gees for one week.

It returned to number one for the weeks of June 16 and June 23.

The single was Donna Summer's second number one, after "MacArthur Park".

What Grammy did Hot Stuff win?

"Hot Stuff" won the Grammy Award for Best Female Rock Vocal Performance at the 1980 ceremony, the first time that category was awarded.

Donna Summer won it ahead of Rickie Lee Jones, Tanya Tucker, Carly Simon and Bonnie Raitt.

This site's own year-end Billboard data records it as the first disco record to win a rock Grammy.

What album is Hot Stuff on?

"Hot Stuff" is the lead single from "Bad Girls", Donna Summer's seventh studio album, released by Casablanca Records in April 1979.

"Bad Girls" was a double album, and its title track also reached number one on the Hot 100.

Rolling Stone later called it the only great disco album other than the "Saturday Night Fever" soundtrack.

Was Hot Stuff written for Donna Summer?

"Hot Stuff" was written by Pete Bellotte, Harold Faltermeyer and Keith Forsey, not by Donna Summer or Giorgio Moroder.

The song was reportedly shown to other singers first, including Cher, before Summer recorded it.

Forsey and Faltermeyer went on to shape 1980s pop, co-writing "Flashdance... What a Feeling" and the "Beverly Hills Cop" theme.
