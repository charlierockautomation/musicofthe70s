Village People in the 70s: The Disco Act Everyone Missed

Village People in the 70s is a story most people think they already know.

Everyone can do the arm dance.

Almost nobody knows where it actually came from.

It started with one dancer at a gay club in Greenwich Village, not a marketing meeting.

It nearly ended up as an actual US Navy recruitment ad.

And most of the audience singing along in 1979 had no idea what the group was really about.

This is the version of the story with the layers put back in.

Some of it, they never noticed at all.

One Dancer at a Gay Club Started It All

Village People in the 70s didn't start with a business plan.

It started with French producer Jacques Morali noticing one dancer.

Felipe Rose was working at The Anvil, a gay club in New York, dressed in full Native American regalia.

That wasn't a random costume choice.

Rose's father was Lakota Sioux, and the look reflected his own heritage.

Morali saw him and had an idea.

Instead of one striking dancer, why not build a whole group of archetypal characters?

Rose became the first recruit.

Morali and co-producer Henri Belolo built the rest of the lineup around him.

The full group came together in 1977.

What looked from the outside like a novelty act started as one specific person's real identity.

That's the pattern that repeats through the entire story of Village People in the 70s.

Something read as costume and spectacle on the surface almost always had a real, specific root underneath it.

Six Macho Archetypes, One Real Message

With Rose in place, Morali and Belolo needed the rest of the group.

They placed an ad in a trade magazine.

It read: "Macho types wanted: must dance and have moustache."

Singer Victor Willis, the cop, joined as co-writer and lead vocalist.

A cowboy, a construction worker, a biker, and a military man rounded out the six.

Every character was drawn straight from gay club iconography of the era.

Morali and Belolo weren't hiding that on purpose, exactly.

They just knew most of mainstream America wouldn't clock it.

They were largely right.

The group became a costumed novelty act to most of its audience, dance moves and mustaches, nothing more.

The producers later described the whole concept as a quiet form of advocacy, arriving in a genuinely conservative era.

Village People in the 70s: Y.M.C.A. Got Bigger Than Anyone Expected

Nothing captures Village People in the 70s better than what happened with "Y.M.C.A."

Morali wrote it after an actual visit to a New York YMCA.

He noticed how diverse and mixed the clientele really was.

The song, released in 1978, spent three weeks at #2 on the Billboard Hot 100.

It landed at #8 on Billboard's own year-end chart for 1979.

The arm-gesture dance that grew up around it is now one of the most universally recognized crowd moments at sporting events worldwide.

Almost none of the stadiums spelling out those letters today are thinking about a gay club anthem from 1978.

That's exactly the gap this whole story lives in.

In the Navy: The Recruitment Ad That Almost Was

"In the Navy" pushed the group's hidden-in-plain-sight identity about as far as it could go.

The US Navy briefly considered using the song as an actual recruitment tool.

They went further than just considering it.

The Navy gave Village People access to real naval vessels to film the music video.

Then someone actually watched the footage and understood the subtext.

The Navy withdrew its association entirely.

By then the access had already been granted and the video already shot.

Commercially, none of that mattered.

"In the Navy" reached #3 on the Hot 100 and #48 on the 1979 year-end chart anyway.

A branch of the US military came closer to co-signing this group than almost anyone realized at the time.

Put the origin story, the ad, "Y.M.C.A.," and this near-miss together and one pattern holds across all of it.

Village People in the 70s kept getting embraced by exactly the mainstream institutions least likely to understand what they were actually endorsing.

That's not an accident of bad marketing.

It's the whole joke, and the whole point, at the same time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who was the first member recruited for Village People?

Felipe Rose was the first member producer Jacques Morali recruited, discovered dancing at the New York club The Anvil in full Native American dress.

Rose chose that look because his father was Lakota Sioux, not as a random costume choice.

Seeing Rose is what gave Morali the idea to build a full group around different archetypal characters.

Why did Jacques Morali form Village People?

Morali, a French producer, wanted to build a disco act around gay archetypal characters after encountering New York's Greenwich Village club scene.

He and co-producer Henri Belolo placed a trade ad reading "Macho types wanted: must dance and have moustache" to fill out the lineup around Rose and singer Victor Willis.

Most mainstream listeners missed the group's intended subtext entirely.

How well did Y.M.C.A. actually chart in 1979?

"Y.M.C.A." spent three weeks at #2 on the Billboard Hot 100 and landed at #8 on the year-end chart for 1979.

Jacques Morali wrote it after visiting a New York YMCA and noticing its diverse clientele.

The arm-gesture dance it inspired became one of the most recognized participation moments at sporting events worldwide.

Why did the US Navy almost use In the Navy as a recruitment song?

The Navy briefly considered "In the Navy" as a recruitment tool and gave Village People access to actual naval vessels to film the video.

The Navy withdrew its association once it understood the song's subtext, after already granting that access.

Despite losing that endorsement, the song still reached #3 on the Hot 100 and #48 on the 1979 year-end chart.

Did Village People's audience understand the group's gay subtext in the 70s?

Most mainstream listeners in the late 1970s did not recognize the LGBTQ subtext built into Village People's music and imagery.

The group's archetypal characters, drawn directly from gay club culture, read to much of the public as straightforward costumed novelty.

Producers Morali and Belolo have described the concept as a form of advocacy during a conservative era.
