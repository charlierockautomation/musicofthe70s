Willie Nelson: The Songwriter Who Became the Star

Willie Nelson spent most of the 1960s writing hit songs for other people to sing.

He wrote "Crazy" for Patsy Cline and "Hello Walls" for Faron Young.

Both were real chart hits, years before he had one of his own as a performer.

By 1970 he was still a mid-level Nashville act, respected as a writer but stuck as a singer.

A house fire, a move to Texas, and one stripped-down concept album changed that inside a few short years.

Most write-ups treat his 70s rise as a straight line toward outlaw stardom.

This one follows the actual reversal, the ghostwriter who became the star, in the order it really happened.

The Songwriter Nobody Called a Star

Two Songs That Made Nashville Take Notice

He moved to Nashville in the late 1950s hoping to sell songs, not sing them himself.

Patsy Cline recorded his song "Crazy" in 1961, and it crossed over from country radio onto the pop charts.

Faron Young topped the country chart that same year with another of his songs, "Hello Walls."

Both records made him a name inside the industry almost overnight.

A Performing Career That Wouldn't Catch Up

His own singles through the 1960s sold modestly and rarely cracked the upper reaches of the country chart.

He signed with RCA Victor in 1965 and released album after album with little commercial reward.

Producers kept him inside Nashville's polished, string-heavy production style, a sound that never quite fit his phrasing.

By the start of the new decade he had written for stars but still wasn't one himself.

The Fire That Sent Him Home to Texas

The Night His Ridgetop House Burned

Willie Nelson was living on a farm in Ridgetop, Tennessee, just outside Nashville, as the 1960s ended.

On December 23, 1969, he came home from a Christmas party to find the house on fire.

He ran back inside the burning building to save what mattered most to him.

He pulled out his guitar, a Martin N-20 he had bought unseen that same year for 750 dollars.

He called it Trigger.

Why He Chose Austin Over Nashville

The fire pushed him to reconsider a career that had stalled inside Nashville's system for over a decade.

He relocated to Austin by 1972, drawn to a scene that mixed country crowds with rock and folk fans.

That younger, looser audience responded to his phrasing in a way Nashville radio never had.

He founded his own annual Fourth of July Picnic festival there in 1973.

An earlier attempt at a similar event, the Dripping Springs Reunion, had already fallen apart.

Shotgun Willie and the Deal Nashville Wouldn't Give Him

Signing With Atlantic Records

Willie Nelson signed with Atlantic Records in 1972, becoming one of the label's first country signings.

The deal gave him room to record with his own touring band instead of Nashville session players.

His 1973 album Shotgun Willie stripped away the string sections and vocal choruses that had padded his earlier records.

Critics and longtime fans heard it as the sound he had always wanted to make.

Moving On to Columbia for Full Control

Atlantic shut down its country division not long after, and he moved to Columbia Records in 1975.

Columbia gave him something almost unheard of for a country artist at the time.

He got full creative control over his own recordings.

That clause let him make his next album exactly the way he wanted.

No label had ever given him that much say before.

It set up the biggest turning point of his career.

Red Headed Stranger and His First Number One

A Concept Album Columbia Almost Rejected

Willie Nelson used his new creative control to record Red Headed Stranger in 1975.

It was a concept album about a preacher on the run after a killing.

He recorded it sparse, mostly just his guitar, piano, and voice.

Almost none of Nashville's usual studio production made it onto the tape.

Columbia executives reportedly thought the stripped-down tapes sounded like an unfinished demo.

He released it as recorded anyway.

Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain Becomes His First Number One

The album became his first to reach number one on Billboard's country albums chart.

Its lead single was "Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain," a cover of a 1945 Fred Rose song.

It wasn't one of his own compositions.

That single hit number one on Billboard's country chart in October 1975.

It was his very first chart-topper as a performer.

It arrived almost fifteen years after "Crazy" had made him famous as someone else's hitmaker.

Wanted! The Outlaws and Country's First Platinum Record

In January 1976 he also appeared on Wanted! The Outlaws, alongside Waylon Jennings, Jessi Colter, and Tompall Glaser.

That compilation became the first country album ever certified platinum.

Every Number One Country Hit Willie Nelson Had in the 70s

Billboard's own weekly country chart data confirms six number one singles carrying his name across the decade.

Four of those were solo hits.

  • "Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain" (October 1975): his first number one as a performer, two weeks at the top.
  • "If You've Got the Money I've Got the Time" (September 1976): a single week at number one.
  • "Mammas Don't Let Your Babies Grow Up to Be Cowboys" (March 1978): a duet with Waylon Jennings, four weeks at number one.
  • "Georgia on My Mind" (June 1978): a cover from his pop-standards album Stardust.
  • "Blue Skies" (September 1978): a second Stardust cover to reach the top.
  • "Heartbreak Hotel" (September 1979): a duet with Leon Russell, an Elvis Presley cover reworked as a country single.

He was one of only two artists to take three different singles to number one in 1978 alone.

Stardust, the pop-standards album behind two of those hits, kept selling for years.

It went on to spend more than ten years on the country albums chart.

No other artist of the decade matched that run of chart-toppers.

None did it while also rewriting what a country record could sound like.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did Willie Nelson get his first number one country hit?

Willie Nelson's first number one country single was "Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain."

It topped Billboard's country chart in October 1975.

The song came from his concept album Red Headed Stranger.

Columbia executives almost rejected the stripped-down record as an unfinished demo.

He had recorded as a performer for nearly two decades before that first chart-topper arrived.

The single held the top spot for two weeks.

Did Willie Nelson write songs for other singers before his own success?

Willie Nelson wrote two major country standards for other artists years before his own performing career took off.

Patsy Cline had a crossover hit with his song "Crazy" in 1961.

Faron Young topped the country chart that same year with his song "Hello Walls."

Both songs made Nelson a respected Nashville songwriter long before he had a hit of his own as a singer.

Why did Willie Nelson move to Austin, Texas in the 1970s?

Willie Nelson moved to Austin after his Ridgetop, Tennessee home burned down on December 23, 1969.

That fire pushed him to reconsider his life in Nashville.

He relocated to Texas by 1972, finding a receptive, younger crowd mixing country with rock and folk fans.

The Austin scene became the launching point for the outlaw country movement.

He founded his annual Fourth of July Picnic festival there in 1973.

What is the story behind Willie Nelson's guitar Trigger?

Trigger is the nickname Willie Nelson gave his Martin N-20 classical guitar.

He bought it unseen for 750 dollars in 1969, after his previous guitar was damaged.

He ran into the burning home that year to save it.

Trigger has a distinctive hole worn through its body from decades of his fingerpicking.

He has played the same guitar on nearly every recording and show since.

How many number one country hits did Willie Nelson have in the 1970s?

Willie Nelson scored four solo number one country singles in the 1970s.

That's based on Billboard's own weekly country chart data.

He added two more chart-toppers as a duet artist, one with Waylon Jennings and one with Leon Russell.

In 1978 alone he was one of only two artists to take three different singles to number one.

That run cemented him as one of the decade's defining country stars.
