Iggy Pop: The 1970s Collapse and Berlin Resurrection

Iggy Pop's own band ended on a Detroit stage in February 1974, drowned out by bottles and jeers instead of applause.

Most pages covering him today jump straight from that collapse to Lust for Life like nothing happened in between.

The real story runs through a biker gang, a canceled career, and a chance friendship with David Bowie in West Berlin.

This site's own chart archive backs up just how far outside the mainstream that whole decade actually played out.

Here's the real arc, from the night the Stooges broke up to the album that brought Iggy Pop back.

How Iggy Pop and the Stooges Actually Broke Up

Two Albums Nobody Bought at the Time

Iggy Pop was born James Newell Osterberg Jr. on April 21, 1947, in Muskegon, Michigan.

He grew up in Ann Arbor and took the nickname Iggy from his first band, a high school group called The Iguanas.

The Stooges formed in Ann Arbor in 1967, with him out front.

Their second album, Fun House, came out in 1970 to almost no commercial notice.

Raw Power followed in 1973, produced with input from David Bowie, their first real connection.

Neither record sold well enough to keep the band together.

The Scorpions Wanted a Fight

The Stooges' final collapse started with a Detroit biker gang, not a record label.

At a show on February 4, 1974, a gang called the Scorpions threw eggs at the stage, upset the band was drawing crowds in what they saw as their turf.

Pop confronted one of them and was knocked out on the spot.

He went on Detroit radio afterward and dared the Scorpions to show up at the band's next gig.

They took him up on it.

Metallic K.O.: The Sound of a Band Ending

The Stooges played what turned out to be their last show on February 9, 1974, at Detroit's Michigan Palace.

Bikers in the crowd pelted the stage with beer bottles, ice, and jelly beans the entire set.

Some of those bottles are audible on tape, shattering against guitar strings mid-song.

A recording of the second half of that show later surfaced as the album Metallic K.O. in 1976.

The Stooges did not play together again for almost thirty years.

The Stage Antics That Predated Punk's Name

Iggy Pop's reputation for chaos on stage started years before anyone called this music punk.

At the Cincinnati Pop Festival on June 13, 1970, he smeared peanut butter across his bare chest mid-performance.

Someone in the crowd threw a full jar back at him.

He then leapt directly into the audience, an early instance of what became known as stage diving.

Footage of the moment was filmed for national television and still circulates as an early punk landmark.

Pop is widely credited as the performer who popularized the move.

None of it was choreographed or planned in advance.

How David Bowie Rebuilt His Career in Berlin

An Arrest That Became a Real Friendship

David Bowie and Iggy Pop were arrested together on a marijuana charge in Rochester, New York, in 1976.

By then Bowie had already spent years championing Pop's music to anyone who would listen.

Bowie brought him along on the Station to Station tour that year, first as a friend, then as a real creative partner.

Bowie helped secure Pop a solo record deal with RCA soon after.

The two of them moved together to West Berlin in late 1976.

The Idiot: Bowie's Guinea Pig

The Idiot, released in March 1977, was Iggy Pop's first true solo album.

Bowie co-wrote and produced the record, using it partly to test ideas he'd apply to his own music next.

The sound leaned atmospheric and cold, a sharp break from the Stooges' raw attack.

It sold better in the UK than anything Pop had released with his old band.

Lust for Life: Straight Rock and Roll Again

Bowie played keyboards on Pop's tour supporting The Idiot that spring.

When the tour ended, the two of them regrouped at Hansa Studio in West Berlin to record a follow-up.

Lust for Life was recorded there in May and June of 1977 and released that September 9.

The touring band from The Idiot's tour became the album's core lineup.

  • David Bowie, keyboards and co-writing
  • Ricky Gardiner, guitar
  • Tony Fox Sales, bass
  • Hunt Sales, drums

Where The Idiot sounded experimental, Lust for Life returned to straightforward, driving rock and roll.

It remains the album most people picture when they think of his real solo sound.

Where This Music Landed on the US Charts

Neither the Stooges nor Iggy Pop's solo work appears anywhere in this site's own validated Billboard Hot 100 archive for the 1970s.

Fun House, Raw Power, The Idiot, and Lust for Life all missed the chart entirely at release.

That's a real gap between influence and commercial performance, not a data error.

This site's own 70s Hard Rock post files Iggy Pop and the Stooges under its proto-metal branch, alongside acts like Grand Funk and Aerosmith.

The 70s Punk Rock post's own 21-artist roster doesn't include either act, since this site tracks them separately under that same hard rock lineage instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Was Iggy Pop still with the Stooges in the 1970s?

Iggy Pop fronted the Stooges through their final breakup on February 9, 1974, at Detroit's Michigan Palace.

He then spent roughly two years outside the music business before David Bowie helped him restart as a solo artist in 1976.

Both count as real 1970s music from him, just released under two very different band names.

What really happened at the Stooges' last show in 1974?

The Stooges played their final show on February 9, 1974, at the Michigan Palace in Detroit after a local biker gang called the Scorpions threatened Iggy Pop days earlier.

Bikers pelted the stage with bottles, ice, and eggs throughout the set.

The chaos was captured live and later released as the album Metallic K.O.

How did David Bowie help Iggy Pop's solo career?

David Bowie helped Iggy Pop secure a solo record deal with RCA and moved with him to West Berlin in late 1976.

Bowie co-wrote and produced both The Idiot in March 1977 and Lust for Life that September, and toured as Pop's keyboardist.

Both albums outsold anything Pop had released with the Stooges.

Did Iggy Pop really invent stage diving?

Iggy Pop is widely credited with popularizing stage diving during a Stooges performance at the Cincinnati Pop Festival on June 13, 1970.

He leapt into the crowd while smeared in peanut butter, thrown at him after he flung a jar into the audience himself.

Footage of the moment still circulates as an early punk landmark.

Did any Iggy Pop or Stooges songs chart on the Billboard Hot 100 in the 70s?

Neither Iggy Pop's solo work nor the Stooges appear anywhere in this site's own validated year-end or weekly Billboard Hot 100 archive for the 1970s.

Real commercial success came later, well outside the decade this site covers.

Their influence on the genre still reached far past whatever the charts recorded at the time.
