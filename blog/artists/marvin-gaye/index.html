Marvin Gaye: The Party Tape That Ended Up in Federal Court

In March 2015, a federal jury ruled that Robin Thicke and Pharrell Williams owed money to Marvin Gaye's estate.

The song at the center of the case was "Got to Give It Up," something Gaye had built almost as a joke back in 1977.

It started as a party he threw inside a Motown studio, not a planned single.

That party tape eventually reshaped how courts think about copying a song.

Marvin Gaye spent most of the decade doing the opposite of throwing songs together, fighting his own label for the right to make exactly the records he wanted.

This is the real, chart-verified story of that decade, from a refused album in 1971 to a federal courtroom decades later.

1971: The Album Berry Gordy Tried to Bury

A Refusal, Then a Strike

Marvin Gaye brought Motown a self-produced concept album about war, poverty, and police brutality in 1970.

Berry Gordy refused to release it, calling it too political and uncommercial for the label.

Gaye refused to record anything else until Motown changed its mind.

Motown gave in, releasing the title single "What's Going On" on January 21, 1971.

It topped Billboard's Soul Singles chart for five straight weeks.

It peaked at number two on the Billboard Hot 100 itself.

This site's own year-end chart data ranks the song 21st for all of 1971, since most of its run happened early in the year before the annual tally closed.

Mercy Mercy Me Keeps the Album's Nerve

The album's second single, "Mercy Mercy Me (The Ecology)," followed that spring.

It tackled pollution and environmental collapse, an unusual subject for a pop single in 1971.

It reached number four on the Billboard Hot 100 and topped the Soul Singles chart.

The site's year-end data ranks it 62nd for 1971, another case where a strong weekly peak didn't translate into a big year-end number.

Marvin Gaye wasn't the only soul artist that decade demanding real creative control from his label.

1973: Let's Get It On Buys Him Creative Control

Gaye's next major album grew out of a real collaboration, not a solo vision.

He co-wrote the title track with gospel songwriter Ed Townsend.

Townsend framed the song as an expression of spiritual love as much as physical love.

"Let's Get It On" hit number one on the Billboard Hot 100 on September 8, 1973.

The site's own year-end chart data ranks it fourth for all of 1973, reflecting how much of the year it spent near the top.

The album became the best-selling record of Gaye's Motown career.

That success finally won him the full creative control Motown had denied him just two years earlier.

1973-1974: A Duet Album Recorded Almost Apart

The Same Method Used for Marvin and Tammi

Motown paired Gaye with Diana Ross for a full duets album in 1973.

The label used the same method it had used for Gaye's earlier duets with Tammi Terrell.

Ross and Gaye recorded most of their vocals in separate sessions.

Engineer Russ Terrana mixed the two vocal tracks together afterward.

A Rocky Start in the Studio

The sessions did not start smoothly.

According to Terrana, Gaye arrived early one day and was smoking marijuana inside the studio.

Ross walked in, saw it, and walked straight back out.

Diana & Marvin still came together and was released on October 26, 1973.

It went on to sell more than a million copies worldwide.

My Mistake Becomes a Real Hit

The album's second single, "My Mistake (Was to Love You)," came out in February 1974.

It reached number 19 on the Billboard Hot 100 and number 15 on the R&B singles chart.

This site's own year-end data ranks it 92nd for 1974, its only appearance on that particular chart.

1977: A Party Becomes a Number-One Record

Recorded Live, Ad-Libs and All

Marvin Gaye hosted an actual party inside a Motown recording studio in 1976.

He kept the tape rolling the whole time.

Guests can still be heard talking and laughing in the finished track.

Gaye built "Got to Give It Up" directly out of that recording instead of scrapping it.

A Number-One Record

"Got to Give It Up (Part 1)" reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in June 1977.

The site's own year-end data ranks it 20th for all of 1977.

It became one of the defining singles of Gaye's career, built almost by accident.

2015: The Party Tape Ends Up in Federal Court

"Got to Give It Up" stayed a well-loved deep cut for decades after 1977.

Then, in 2013, Robin Thicke, Pharrell Williams, and T.I. released "Blurred Lines," the best-selling single of that year.

Marvin Gaye's family argued the song copied the feel of "Got to Give It Up," not its actual melody or lyrics.

A federal jury agreed in March 2015 and awarded the Gaye family $5.3 million in damages.

An appeals court later raised that award to $7.4 million plus half of all future royalties from "Blurred Lines."

The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the verdict in March 2018.

A song built from a house party tape in 1976 ended up reshaping how courts weigh the feel of a song, not just its notes.

Marvin Gaye's Real 1970s Chart Record

Marvin Gaye's decade breaks down into five verified chart entries, each with its own real story behind it.

  • "What's Going On" (1971): Hot 100 peak #2, Soul Singles #1 for five weeks, ranked 21st on the site's 1971 year-end chart.
  • "Mercy Mercy Me (The Ecology)" (1971): Hot 100 peak #4, Soul Singles #1, ranked 62nd on the site's 1971 year-end chart.
  • "Let's Get It On" (1973): Hot 100 #1 on September 8, 1973, ranked 4th on the site's 1973 year-end chart.
  • "My Mistake (Was to Love You)" with Diana Ross (1974): Hot 100 #19, R&B #15, ranked 92nd on the site's 1974 year-end chart.
  • "Got to Give It Up (Part 1)" (1977): Hot 100 #1, ranked 20th on the site's 1977 year-end chart.

Two of those five songs hit number one, and a third reached number two, real numbers behind a decade often remembered only for its two most famous album titles.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Motown almost refuse to release What's Going On?

Berry Gordy believed Marvin Gaye's self-produced protest album was too political and uncommercial for a Motown release.

Gaye refused to record anything else until the label changed its position.

Motown eventually released the title single on January 21, 1971.

It reached number two on the Billboard Hot 100 and topped the Soul Singles chart for five weeks.

What inspired Marvin Gaye's Let's Get It On?

Marvin Gaye co-wrote the title track with gospel songwriter Ed Townsend, who framed the song as an expression of spiritual love as much as physical love.

It reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 on September 8, 1973.

The album became the best-selling record of Gaye's Motown career, and its success won him full creative control over his future work.

Why did Diana Ross and Marvin Gaye record their duet album separately?

Motown used the same approach it had used for Gaye's earlier duets with Tammi Terrell, recording each singer in separate sessions rather than together.

Engineer Russ Terrana mixed the two vocal tracks together afterward.

The sessions had real tension too.

Ross reportedly walked out of the studio at one point over Gaye smoking marijuana inside it.

What is the story behind Got to Give It Up?

Marvin Gaye recorded the song from a tape of an actual party he hosted inside a Motown studio in 1976, keeping the guests' talking and laughter audible in the finished mix.

He built the track directly from that recording.

Released as Got to Give It Up (Part 1), it reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in June 1977.

How is Got to Give It Up connected to the Blurred Lines lawsuit?

A federal jury ruled in March 2015 that Robin Thicke and Pharrell Williams's 2013 hit Blurred Lines infringed on the feel of Marvin Gaye's Got to Give It Up.

The jury awarded Gaye's family $5.3 million in damages.

An appeals court later raised that to $7.4 million plus half of all future royalties, a verdict the Ninth Circuit upheld in 2018.
