Queen Freddie Mercury: Two Nights That Defined Their 70s

Queen Freddie Mercury's whole decade can be traced through two nights that had nothing to do with each other on the surface.

One was a calculated gamble that changed how music gets promoted forever.

The other was an accident, a toothache that nobody planned for.

That accident ended up launching an entire other genre.

Most retrospectives treat Queen as one long rise to arena domination.

The real story has far stranger turns than that.

Here are the two nights, then everything that connects them.

The Toothache That Accidentally Launched Punk

On December 1, 1976, Queen was booked to appear on Today, a live British teatime TV show.

Freddie Mercury needed emergency dental treatment that same day, his first checkup in years.

The band had no choice but to cancel.

EMI's publicist, Eric Hall, scrambled for a replacement act to fill the slot.

He offered up a scruffy, mostly unknown band signed to the same label.

That band was the Sex Pistols.

Host Bill Grundy needled the band on air, daring them to say something outrageous.

Guitarist Steve Jones obliged, live, on British teatime television.

The next morning's tabloids treated it as a national scandal.

Almost overnight, punk went from a small London scene to front-page news across the country.

The Daily Mirror ran the incident under the headline "The Filth and the Fury."

Other tabloids piled on with their own outraged front pages the same week.

None of it would have happened without Freddie Mercury's dentist appointment.

The Sex Pistols' own "God Save the Queen" arrived the following year, riding directly on that notoriety.

The Video Gamble Nobody Else Would Make

A year earlier, Queen made a very different kind of gamble.

"Bohemian Rhapsody" ran nearly six minutes with no chorus and an operatic mid-section.

EMI worried radio would never touch it.

Instead of relying on a normal TV performance to promote it, the band spent roughly four hours filming a dedicated promotional film with director Bruce Gowers.

No rock act had built an entire video around a single song quite like that before.

It's now widely credited as one of the first true music videos.

How Queen Freddie Mercury Actually Began

Before Queen existed, Brian May and Roger Taylor played together in a band called Smile.

Freddie Mercury, still going by his birth name Farrokh Bulsara, was already a friend of the group.

When Smile's singer Tim Staffell left in early 1970, Mercury stepped into the frontman role.

He also pushed hard for a new, bolder name.

Queen was his idea, chosen for its regal, theatrical weight.

The lineup still needed a bass player.

John Deacon joined in February 1971, after the band met him at a London disco.

That completed the four-piece that would record every album of the decade.

Brian May's own guitar had an origin nearly as unusual as the band's name.

He built his instrument, later nicknamed the Red Special, with his father back in 1963.

May used that same homemade guitar on almost every Queen record throughout the 70s.

Around this same period, Mercury adopted his new surname, inspired by Mercury, the ruling planet of his astrological sign.

Different sources describe the exact legal status of that change differently.

What's certain is that Freddie Mercury became his permanent public identity from that point forward.

The Song Built From a Stomp and a Clap

By 1977, Queen's concerts had stopped feeling like a one-way performance.

At a gig at Bingley Hall in Stafford, fans sang along to nearly every song.

As the band left the stage, the crowd broke into its own unplanned rendition of "You'll Never Walk Alone."

Brian May noticed the shift and wanted to build a song around it directly.

He asked himself a simple question: what can an audience actually do without any instruments?

His answer was stomp, clap, and sing.

The band recorded the beat by hand and foot on a drum riser at Wessex Sound Studio.

Roadies joined in, and so did Betty, the studio's tea lady.

May layered different studio delays on each take.

The result sounded like it came from hundreds of people instead of a handful.

That song, We Will Rock You, was built to hand the show back to the crowd that inspired it.

That's the real shape of Queen Freddie Mercury's 1970s, one toothache and one gamble at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How did Queen Freddie Mercury actually form?

Queen Freddie Mercury grew out of an earlier band called Smile, formed by Brian May and Roger Taylor.

When singer Tim Staffell left in early 1970, Mercury, already a friend of the group, stepped in as frontman and pushed for the name Queen.

Bassist John Deacon completed the lineup in February 1971 after the band met him at a London disco.

What happened when Queen canceled their 1976 TV appearance?

Queen pulled out of a December 1, 1976 slot on the British teatime show Today after Freddie Mercury needed emergency dental treatment.

EMI publicist Eric Hall offered the Sex Pistols as a last-minute replacement.

Host Bill Grundy provoked the band into swearing on live television, and the resulting tabloid uproar pushed punk into the national spotlight overnight.

How was We Will Rock You actually written and recorded?

Brian May wrote it after Queen's fans spontaneously sang along at a gig at Bingley Hall in Stafford.

He wanted a song built entirely around what a crowd could do without instruments: stomp, clap, and sing.

The band, their roadies, and even the studio's tea lady recorded the stomp-clap beat by hand and foot, layered with studio delay to sound like hundreds of people.

Why was the Bohemian Rhapsody video considered such a big gamble?

Very few rock acts had built an entire promotional film around a single before, and EMI worried a six-minute song with no chorus would never get radio play at all.

Queen spent roughly four hours filming it with director Bruce Gowers instead of relying on live TV performances.

It's now widely credited as one of the first true music videos.

Did Freddie Mercury legally change his name to Mercury?

Freddie Mercury was born Farrokh Bulsara, and he began using the stage name Mercury as Queen's career took off in the early 1970s.

Multiple accounts of exactly when and how the surname became official conflict with each other, so his birth name is well documented but the precise legal record isn't settled.

Either way, Mercury became his permanent public identity.
