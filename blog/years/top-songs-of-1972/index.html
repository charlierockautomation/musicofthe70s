Top Songs of 1972: The Year's Biggest Hits & Trends

By Charlie · Published July 30, 2026 · Updated October 6, 2026

▶ Jump to the playable list

The top songs of 1972 were led by a ballad that almost didn't become a hit at all.

Roberta Flack's "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" had been sitting on her 1969 debut album.

It sat there for three years before it suddenly exploded into the biggest song of the year.

Beyond that number one, 1972 was a year of real soul dominance.

It was also full of strange chart coincidences.

A wave of artists each landed exactly two songs on the year-end list.

This is a closer look at the artists and stories behind the top songs of 1972.

Roberta Flack's Slow-Burn Ballad Topped the Top Songs of 1972

Billboard's Year-End Hot 100 named "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" the top song of 1972.

Its path to that spot was anything but typical.

Roberta Flack had recorded the song back in 1969 for her debut album.

It took years and an unexpected boost to turn it into a genuine phenomenon.

The song was originally written in 1957 by British singer-songwriter Ewan MacColl.

It found new life years later in an unexpected way.

Director Clint Eastwood featured Flack's version prominently in his film "Play Misty for Me."

That placement is widely credited with driving the song's explosion in popularity.

A song that once sat quietly on a debut album suddenly had a second life.

That slow build paid off in a big way.

The song went on to win Grammys for both Record of the Year and Song of the Year.

Al Green Became 1972's Most Charted Artist

While Flack held the top spot, no artist placed more songs on the year-end list than Al Green.

Three separate Al Green singles made the 1972 chart, more than any other act that year.

His run that year included three singles.

  • "Let's Stay Together"
  • "Look What You Done for Me"
  • "I'm Still in Love with You"

That stretch cemented him as one of soul music's defining voices of the early 70s.

That kind of consistency, three genuine hits in a single year, stands out.

It's a clear sign of just how dominant Green's sound was during this stretch of the decade.

That dominance is part of why the top songs of 1972 still get discussed today.

The Same Song, Two Different Acts

One of the strangest quirks of 1972's chart involved a single song.

It made the year-end list twice, under two completely different names.

"I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing (In Perfect Harmony)" made the year-end list for both acts.

The New Seekers and The Hillside Singers each recorded their own version.

Both versions charted in the same year.

It remains one of the more unusual footnotes in Billboard chart history.

That kind of overlap was rare even by the standards of the era.

It says something about how much the song's message resonated with listeners.

Two competing versions still found an audience at the same time.

A Year Full of Two-Hit Wonders

1972 was also a year where landing exactly two hits on the year-end list was almost its own trend.

Several major names hit that mark at once.

  • Michael Jackson, with "Ben" and "Rockin' Robin"
  • Harry Nilsson, with "Without You" and "Coconut"
  • Don McLean, with "American Pie" and "Vincent"
  • Bill Withers, with "Lean on Me" and "Use Me"
  • Three Dog Night, with "Black and White" and "Never Been to Spain"
  • Joe Simon, with "Drowning in the Sea of Love" and "Power of Love"

That spread, six different artists all landing precisely two hits, is striking.

It shows just how wide open the middle of the chart was that year.

Success was shared across soul, pop, and singer-songwriter acts alike.

That balance is a big part of what makes the top songs of 1972 worth revisiting.

Instrumentals Found Real Chart Success

Not every hit song of 1972 needed lyrics to connect with listeners.

Instrumental tracks performed unusually well on the year-end chart that year.

That run was led by "Popcorn" by Hot Butter and "Scorpio" by Dennis Coffey.

Both tracks proved that a strong hook and a distinctive sound could carry a song into the year-end conversation.

That's just as true as it is for a memorable vocal performance.

Neither song needed a single lyric to become a genuine year-end hit.

That range says a lot about the top songs of 1972 as a whole.
