70s Progressive Rock: The Genre That Barely Had Hits

Ask ten classic rock fans to name a 70s hit single, and you'll get ten different answers in seconds.

Ask the same fans to name a 70s progressive rock hit single, and most will stall out.

That's not an accident.

This site's own chart archive backs it up: prog rock is the smallest genre bucket on record here, just 11 charting entries across the whole decade.

The genre built its real legacy somewhere else entirely, on the album.

What Actually Counts as 70s Progressive Rock

A Genre Built on Ambition

70s progressive rock chased artistic scale most rock bands never attempted.

Musicians folded classical structure, jazz improvisation, and folk melody directly into rock's basic format.

Songs stretched past ten minutes without much concern for radio.

Full albums, not singles, became the genre's real creative unit.

This site's own artist database tracks 50 real 70s progressive rock acts built on that same ambition.

The Real Subgenre Breakdown

No single sound defines the genre, even inside that roster of 50.

Symphonic prog makes up the largest share, bands leaning on orchestral keyboards and multi-part suites.

Several other real scenes sit alongside it.

  • Canterbury scene: Soft Machine, Caravan, and Gong, all tracing back to the same English city
  • Krautrock: Can and Tangerine Dream, West Germany's own experimental rock scene
  • Italian prog: Banco del Mutuo Soccorso, Premiata Forneria Marconi, and Goblin
  • Jazz-rock: Brand X and Colosseum, built around real improvisation
  • Zeuhl: Magma, a scene with exactly one true originator

That range is exactly why most "best of prog" lists barely scratch the surface.

Why Prog Rock Barely Had Hit Singles

The Numbers Behind the Album-First Reputation

The chart numbers here are genuinely stark.

This site's own year-end chart archive tags only 11 songs as progressive rock across all of 1970 through 1979.

Compare that to 58 for funk, 28 for hard rock, and 165 for soft rock, and the gap is obvious.

Prog rock is the smallest genre bucket in this site's entire chart archive, smaller even than R&B.

A few songs did break through anyway.

The Moody Blues' "Nights in White Satin" reached the Hot 100 in 1972, years after its original 1967 release.

Yes charted with "Roundabout" that same year.

Mike Oldfield's "Tubular Bells" and Pink Floyd's "Money" both appeared on the chart too.

Those songs are the exceptions, not the rule.

Most of the genre's biggest artists never chased the format at all.

The Concept Albums That Built Its Real Legacy

Thick as a Brick: A Parody That Became a #1

Jethro Tull built the genre's strangest concept album almost as a joke.

Frontman Ian Anderson had grown tired of critics calling the band's prior album a concept record.

So he set out to write "the mother of all concept albums" on purpose, as a parody.

The result, 1972's Thick as a Brick, is one continuous piece of music spread across both sides of the LP.

The album's liner notes credited the lyrics to Gerald Bostock, an eight-year-old poetry prodigy.

Bostock never existed.

Anderson invented him entirely, down to the fake disqualified poetry contest that supposedly inspired the album.

Critics reacted with real confusion.

Audiences didn't care either way.

Thick as a Brick became Jethro Tull's first number-one album on the Billboard 200.

The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway: Theater as Rock Show

Genesis pushed the concept album into full theater.

Their 1974 double album The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway followed Rael, a surreal, half-real New York street kid, through an underground fever dream.

Frontman Peter Gabriel performed the album's tour in elaborate costumes and masks built for each character in the story.

The photo at the top of this page captures one of those nights, Chicago, November 1974.

The tour ran 102 dates across North America and Europe.

It was also Gabriel's last with the band.

He left Genesis shortly after the tour wrapped, worn down by the album's own creative demands.

Queen pushed a different kind of theatrical ambition into a single that same decade.

The International Scenes Most Lists Skip

Most "best of 70s prog" coverage sticks to a handful of British and American bands.

Real scenes outside that circle rarely get mentioned at all.

Magma's Invented Language

French drummer Christian Vander built an entire universe around Magma.

He founded the band in Paris in 1969 after a vision of humanity's ecological future disturbed him deeply.

Vander invented a full constructed language for the band, called Kobaïan.

Most of Magma's lyrics are sung entirely in it.

Their debut album tells the story of a group fleeing a dying Earth to settle on the planet Kobaïa.

The style Magma pioneered eventually got its own name, zeuhl, Kobaïan for "celestial."

Italy's Horror-Soundtrack Prog

Italy built one of the richest progressive rock scenes outside Britain.

Banco del Mutuo Soccorso and Premiata Forneria Marconi led the country's symphonic side through the early 70s.

One Italian band found an entirely different outlet for the sound.

Goblin scored director Dario Argento's 1977 horror film Suspiria, one of the genre's most recognizable soundtracks.

The band used unconventional instruments, including a bouzouki tuned specifically to sound wrong, to build the film's unsettling atmosphere.

It remains one of prog rock's strangest, most effective side careers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What defines 70s progressive rock?

70s progressive rock is built on long-form composition, folding classical, jazz, and folk influences into rock's basic structure.

The genre splits into real, distinct scenes, including symphonic prog, the Canterbury sound, Krautrock, Italian prog, and jazz-rock, each with its own sonic identity.

Most bands favored full albums and extended pieces over the three-minute single.

Did any 70s progressive rock songs become hit singles?

A small handful of 70s progressive rock songs did chart, including the Moody Blues' Nights in White Satin, Yes's Roundabout, and Mike Oldfield's Tubular Bells.

This site's own chart archive shows only 11 progressive rock entries across the entire decade, the smallest count of any genre covered here.

Albums, not singles, carried the genre's real commercial weight.

What's a concept album, and why did prog rock rely on them?

A concept album ties every track to one continuous story, theme, or musical idea, rather than collecting unrelated songs.

Progressive rock leaned on the format because its musicians wanted the room a single side of vinyl could give a long composition.

Jethro Tull's Thick as a Brick and Genesis's The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway both built entire albums around one unbroken idea.

What's the difference between prog rock and Krautrock?

Krautrock refers specifically to the experimental rock scene that grew out of West Germany in the late 1960s and 1970s, led by bands like Can and Tangerine Dream.

It shares progressive rock's love of long-form composition but leans harder on repetition, tape experimentation, and electronic texture.

Most fans treat it as a genuinely separate scene that overlapped with prog rather than a subgenre of it.

Who are the essential 70s progressive rock artists?

Yes, Pink Floyd, Genesis, King Crimson, and Emerson, Lake & Palmer anchor the genre's best-known symphonic core.

Jethro Tull, Camel, and Gentle Giant carried its more eclectic, folk-leaning side.

Beyond the familiar names, Can, Soft Machine, and Magma built entire scenes of their own that rarely make a standard best-of list.
