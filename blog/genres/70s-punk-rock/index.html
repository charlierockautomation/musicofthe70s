70s Punk Rock: The Genre With Zero Chart Hits

70s punk rock barely touched the American charts, and this site's own data proves it in a way no top-ten list can.

Thirty-five artists sit inside this site's own punk archive.

Only 21 of them are actually punk.

The other 14 are tagged new wave, and that split explains something real.

Every single artist who ever charted from this bucket carries the new-wave tag, not the punk one.

What Counts as 70s Punk Rock

Twenty-One Real Artists, Six Real Sounds

70s punk rock covers a smaller, more specific group than most best-of lists suggest.

This site's own artist database tags 35 acts under punk.

Only 21 of them are real punk by subgenre.

British punk leads that real group with 6 acts, tied with post-punk's own 6.

Art-punk follows with 4, then american-punk and pop-punk with 2 apiece.

Mod-revival trails with just 1, The Jam carrying that tag alone.

The Other 14 Are New Wave, Not Punk

The same source file also holds 14 artists tagged new wave, synth-pop, or pub-rock.

Blondie, Devo, The Cars, The Police, and The Pretenders all sit in that group.

So does Talking Heads, already covered in this site's own Talking Heads Artists post.

That post tells their real CBGB debut story in full, so this one won't repeat it.

None of those 14 acts get counted as real punk here.

Iggy Pop and the Stooges don't appear in this 35-act bucket at all, filed instead under this site's own hard rock data as proto-metal.

This site's own Iggy Pop post covers punk's real root story from before punk had a name.

The Chart Data Draws Its Own Line

This site's own chart archive settles the punk-versus-new-wave question by accident.

Only one artist from the entire 35-act bucket ever charted in this site's own year-end archive.

That artist is Blondie, with "Heart of Glass" in 1979.

Blondie carries the new-wave tag, not punk.

Every real punk act in this bucket, all 21 of them, has zero entries in this site's own Hot 100 archive.

The data draws the same line critics have argued about for decades, without anyone asking it to.

A UK Chart Story This Archive Doesn't Track

That zero doesn't mean punk never touched a chart at all.

This site's own archive tracks the US Billboard Hot 100, not the UK singles chart.

The Sex Pistols' "God Save the Queen" hit number two in Britain during the week of the Queen's Silver Jubilee in 1977.

The BBC had already banned the song from radio play entirely.

Rod Stewart's "I Don't Want to Talk About It" held the real number one spot that week instead.

Rumors that the chart was quietly manipulated to keep punk out of first place have followed the story ever since.

CBGB Started as a Country Bar

CBGB's own name gives away how far its original plan drifted.

Owner Hilly Kristal opened the club in December 1973.

CBGB stood for Country, BlueGrass, and Blues, the sound he actually wanted to book.

He later added OMFUG to the sign, short for Other Music For Uplifting Gourmandizers.

Real country acts never filled the room the way Kristal hoped.

Television Lied Their Way Into the Booking

One of CBGB's first rock bookings happened because a band lied about what it played.

Television told Kristal they played country music to get a slot on the calendar.

They didn't.

That first booking helped turn CBGB into the room punk needed.

The Ramones Played There Seventy Times in One Year

Formed in Forest Hills, Queens in 1974, the Ramones built their whole identity around speed.

Their first show happened on March 30, 1974, at a different Manhattan space entirely.

Their CBGB debut followed that August.

The band played the club more than seventy times before the year was out.

All four members adopted the same stage surname, Ramone, though none of them were actually related.

Songs rarely passed the two-minute mark.

Britain's Scene Ran on Scandal, Not Sales

The Sex Pistols became famous nationwide before most of Britain ever heard a note.

On December 1, 1976, the band appeared on the early-evening show Today with host Bill Grundy.

Grundy pushed band members to swear on live television, and they did.

Newspapers ran the story under headlines like "The Filth and the Fury" the very next morning.

Grundy was suspended within days, and his own show was cancelled two months later.

The Sex Pistols were suddenly a household name for reasons that had nothing to do with a record.

Why Punk Mattered Without a Hit Single

None of this required a chart placement to matter.

Independent labels pressed and distributed records the major labels wouldn't touch.

Bands booked their own tours and printed their own flyers.

That do-it-yourself model became punk's real lasting export.

Scenes in Los Angeles, Boston, and Washington, D.C. copied it within a few years.

The sound reshaped what came right after it, too.

Several acts on this site's own new-wave list, including Blondie, built their sound directly on ground punk cleared first.

70s punk rock changed the decade that followed it more than it ever changed a chart.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as 70s punk rock on this site?

70s punk rock on this site covers 21 real artists split across six subgenres: british-punk, post-punk, art-punk, american-punk, pop-punk, and mod-revival.

A separate group of 14 new-wave, synth-pop, and pub-rock acts sits in the same source data but isn't counted as real punk here.

The split is based on each artist's own tagged subgenre, not just a shared punk label.

Did any 70s punk rock songs chart in the US?

Real punk never cracked this site's own Hot 100 archive.

Only one artist from the entire 35-act source bucket charted at all, Blondie's "Heart of Glass" in 1979, and Blondie carries the new-wave tag, not punk.

Every one of the 21 real punk acts has zero entries in this site's own chart data.

Did the Sex Pistols ever have a number-one hit?

Not on the US chart this site tracks, though one single came close in the UK.

"God Save the Queen" peaked at number two on the British singles chart during the Queen's 1977 Silver Jubilee week, despite a full BBC radio ban.

Rod Stewart's "I Don't Want to Talk About It" held the actual top spot that week.

What was CBGB before it became a punk club?

CBGB started out built around country, bluegrass, and blues, the three styles its own name stands for.

Owner Hilly Kristal opened it in December 1973 with that sound in mind.

Real country acts never filled the room, and rock bands like Television and the Ramones took over the booking calendar instead.

Why does 70s punk rock matter if it barely charted?

Its real impact happened outside any chart entirely.

Independent labels and self-booked tours gave punk bands a way around the major-label system altogether, and that do-it-yourself model spread to scenes in Los Angeles, Boston, and Washington, D.C. within a few years.

Several new-wave acts that did chart, including Blondie, built their sound directly on ground punk cleared first.
