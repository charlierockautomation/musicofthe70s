Pop Songs of 70s: 8 Real Number Ones Hiding in the Data

Pop songs of 70s get flattened into one lazy stereotype: bubblegum, disposable, and forgettable.

This site's own chart archive tells a very different story.

Pop is the single largest genre in that archive.

It logs 230 charting entries across the decade, ahead of soul's 185 and soft rock's 165.

Buried inside that data are eight real number-one hits that modest year-end rankings make easy to overlook entirely.

TV-built teen idols, one-and-done wonders, and a rock star who barely slowed down all share this same bucket.

What Pop Songs of 70s Actually Covers

A Genre Built From Fourteen Different Sounds

Pop songs of 70s covers real breadth, not one single sound.

This site's own artist database tracks 50 real acts inside the pop-crossover bucket.

Those 50 artists split across 14 different subgenre tags.

That's more variety than any other genre bucket built here so far.

Pop-rock and teen-pop lead the roster, each claiming roughly a fifth of the acts.

Glam-pop sits right behind them, seven artists strong.

Their sound leans as much toward Bowie and Elton John as it does toward the Osmonds.

Soft-pop, bubblegum-pop, and adult contemporary round out the rest.

Tighter pockets of folk-pop and country-pop follow, plus one lone latin-pop act in Julio Iglesias.

A Few Names Already Show Up Elsewhere on This Site

A handful of artists in this bucket overlap with other genres this site already covers.

Elton John and Dr. Hook both carry glam-pop and soft-pop tags here.

Both also sit inside this site's own 70s Soft Rock artist data.

David Bowie gets a brief mention in the site's 70s Rock overview too.

It credits him as the genre's most restless pioneer.

Neither of those posts spends real time on their pop chart runs.

That gap is exactly what this post fills instead.

How TV Screens Built the Decade's Teen Idols

The Partridge Family Started as Fiction and Became a Real Band

The Partridge Family charted on the real Hot 100 before the actors could even play their own instruments.

ABC built the 1970 sitcom around a fictional family band fronted by Shirley Jones and a teenage David Cassidy.

Session musicians played most of the instrumental parts on the show's early singles.

Cassidy sang lead himself, and the arrangement worked well enough to land in this site's own archive twice.

The show ran four seasons, and Cassidy's own teen-idol career outlasted it by years.

Bay City Rollers Turned Tartan Into a TV Franchise

Bay City Rollers built teen mania on both sides of the Atlantic through television, not just records.

Their own British series, Shang-a-Lang, ran for twenty episodes in 1975.

It put the band's tartan-and-platform look in front of a live studio audience of teenage fans every week.

By late 1978, NBC had renamed its Krofft Superstar Hour into The Bay City Rollers Show.

The new version was built entirely around the band.

The photo at the top of this page captures that same era.

Five band members pose in matching plaid, photographed at Amsterdam's Schiphol Airport in February 1976.

Donny and Marie, Shaun Cassidy, and the Hardy Boys Pipeline

ABC leaned on the same playbook twice more before the decade ended.

Donny and Marie Osmond hosted their own prime-time variety show from January 1976 through January 1979.

It was built directly around songs from their own real chart careers.

Shaun Cassidy, David Cassidy's half-brother through their father Jack Cassidy, starred in his own TV mystery series.

That series, The Hardy Boys/Nancy Drew Mysteries, premiered in 1977.

His cover of "Da Doo Ron Ron" turned into a real chart-topper that same year.

John Travolta followed a similar path from a different angle.

He moved from the sitcom Welcome Back, Kotter into a real Top 10 hit of his own, "Let Her In."

Grease made him a movie star soon after.

Pop Songs of 70s: Eight Real Number Ones Hiding in Plain Sight

This site's own year-end archive ranks each song by its best week of that calendar year.

It doesn't track the song's all-time Billboard peak.

That distinction hides something real.

Eight different acts in this bucket reached number one on the actual weekly Billboard Hot 100.

Every one of them still ranks outside the top ten in this site's own year-end list.

Two One-Week Wonders on Opposite Ends of the Decade

Looking Glass and Paper Lace both grabbed number one for exactly one week, years apart.

Looking Glass hit number one with "Brandy (You're a Fine Girl)" on August 26, 1972.

Paper Lace followed two years later with "The Night Chicago Died," number one on August 17, 1974.

Both bands are remembered today mostly as one-hit wonders.

For one week apiece, they genuinely topped the entire American singles chart.

A Movie Theme That Beat Out Barbra Streisand

Maureen McGovern almost never got the chance to record "The Morning After" at all.

The Poseidon Adventure's producers first offered the love theme to Barbra Streisand, who turned it down.

20th Century Records head Russ Regan then remembered a demo tape from a secretary.

She sang folk music part time.

That secretary was McGovern, hired sight unseen to record the song.

"The Morning After" spent two weeks at number one in August 1973.

It also won the Academy Award for Best Original Song.

1976 Alone Produced Two of the Eight

A single year inside this decade accounts for a quarter of the whole reveal.

Bay City Rollers' "Saturday Night" spent one week at number one that January, the band's only US chart-topper.

Starland Vocal Band followed that July with "Afternoon Delight."

It spent two weeks at number one and won a Grammy for Best New Artist.

Both songs still rank outside this site's own year-end top forty.

Shaun Cassidy Made Real Chart History With His Own Mother

Shaun Cassidy's version of "Da Doo Ron Ron" hit number one on July 16, 1977.

That made Cassidy and his mother, Shirley Jones, chart history.

They became the first mother and son to each land a Billboard number one of their own.

Jones had topped the same chart with the Partridge Family's "I Think I Love You" back in 1970.

Elton John and Frankie Valli Both Hit Number One From Deep in the Bucket

Two of the bucket's biggest names each landed a number one that barely shows up here.

Elton John's "Island Girl" spent three weeks at number one in the fall of 1975.

Frankie Valli's "Grease," the movie's title theme, spent two weeks at number one in August 1978.

Both entries sit well outside the top ten of this site's own archive for their respective years.

Elton John Out-Charted Everyone Else in the Bucket

One artist in this bucket never needed a TV show or a single fluke week at number one.

Elton John charted twelve separate times in this site's own pop-crossover archive across the decade.

That's more than double Andy Gibb's five entries, the bucket's next-closest act.

Gibb's own "Shadow Dancing" hit number one in 1978.

That story is already covered in this site's Disco Music of the 70s post, alongside his brothers' own chart run.

"Rocket Man," "Crocodile Rock," "Daniel," "Bennie and the Jets," and "Philadelphia Freedom" all come from that same twelve-song run.

His glam-pop tag here sits right alongside artists a fraction of his commercial size.

That's proof this bucket never separated by fame, only by sound.

That kind of range is exactly what makes pop songs of 70s worth taking seriously today.

Most of These Acts Only Got One Real Shot

Thirty-three of this bucket's 50 artists actually charted in this site's own archive at all.

Twenty-one of those 33, close to two-thirds, charted exactly once.

Starland Vocal Band is the clearest example.

Their only entry in this archive is the same "Afternoon Delight" that hit number one.

Mungo Jerry, Dave Edmunds, Ace, Cliff Richard, Engelbert Humperdinck, and several others share that same one-and-done pattern.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as pop songs of 70s on this site?

Pop songs of 70s on this site cover 50 real crossover artists spanning 14 different subgenres.

That range runs from glam-pop to bubblegum-pop to adult contemporary.

The bucket includes teen idols built by television and rock stars like Elton John who charted repeatedly.

It also includes one-and-done acts that never returned to the Hot 100.

It's defined by sound, not by fame or chart size.

How many number-one hits came from this pop crossover bucket?

Eight different acts in this bucket reached number one on the real weekly Billboard Hot 100.

They are Looking Glass, Maureen McGovern, Paper Lace, Bay City Rollers, Starland Vocal Band, Shaun Cassidy, Elton John, and Frankie Valli.

Every one of those songs ranks outside the top ten in this site's own year-end chart archive.

That archive tracks each song's best week of the year, not its all-time peak.

Which pop crossover artist charted the most in the 70s?

Elton John charted twelve separate times inside this site's pop-crossover archive.

That's more than double any other artist in the bucket.

Andy Gibb is the closest runner-up with five entries.

Songs like Rocket Man, Bennie and the Jets, and Philadelphia Freedom all come from that same run.

That run spans nearly the entire decade.

Were most 70s pop crossover artists one-hit wonders?

Most of them were, at least inside this site's own archive.

Thirty-three of the bucket's 50 artists charted at all, and 21 of those 33 charted exactly once.

Starland Vocal Band fits that pattern exactly: their single archive entry, Afternoon Delight, happened to reach number one anyway.

How did television shape 70s pop crossover acts?

Television built several of this bucket's biggest names directly.

The Partridge Family started as a fictional TV band before its songs became real hits.

Bay City Rollers, Donny and Marie Osmond, and Shaun Cassidy all had their own TV shows running alongside their chart careers.

John Travolta followed the same pipeline from Welcome Back, Kotter.
